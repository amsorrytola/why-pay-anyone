Abstract

If an AI system can do a task for a few cents, an employer will not pay a person much more than that to do it. This project studies that wage ceiling and the forces that decide whether people still earn well under it. I measure how fast the price of a fixed level of AI capability is falling, where AI is used across occupations, whether firm adoption shows up in hiring, and how concentrated the market for AI is. I then take the evidence into a task-based labour model, a growth model with AI capital and a two-group demand model, and add an India lens built on the 2025 Periodic Labour Force Survey microdata. The cheapest price of GPT-4-level capability fell 1,321-fold in 38 months, halving every 3.8 months. Yet the price is rarely what binds: what AI can do, how well tasks substitute for each other, competition among AI sellers and the ownership of AI income decide wages and the labour share. In the models the real wage does not fall when firms adopt AI freely; the labour share falls if compute substitutes for labour. Only 6% of Indian workers hold jobs above the US top-third exposure line, against 33% in the US. All results are exploratory, and every number can be reproduced from public code.

Keywords: artificial intelligence, task-based model, wage ceiling, labour share, price index, market concentration, India, PLFS.

1Question and objectives

In September 2023 the cheapest model at about GPT-4 level cost $37.50 per million tokens; in November 2026 a model at least that capable cost $0.03. When a substitute for labour becomes 1,321 times cheaper, the wage for the tasks it can do is capped by its cost. Index tasks by i, let a person do task i at cost weθi, where w is the wage and e−θi the person’s relative productivity, and let AI do it at cost R. A person keeps the task only if

w ≤ R e−θi.(1)

As R falls, the ceiling falls. Whether people still earn well then depends on four forces: comparative advantage while compute is scarce (Ricardo), new tasks that reinstate labour (Acemoglu and Restrepo, 2019), whether demand holds up when income moves from wages to profits (Say against Keynes), and who owns the AI (market power and distribution).

Objectives. To measure each force with public data and a standard model, and to deliver the work so that a reader can change the assumptions and see the results: (i) the price of capability and its elasticity of demand; (ii) where AI is used; (iii) whether adoption is visible in hiring; (iv) when cheap AI lowers pay in a task model; (v) concentration among AI sellers; (vi) consequences for growth, the labour share and policy; (vii) the exposure of Indian workers, from official microdata.

Why this approach. Each method answers one question with the least structure it needs. Prices are tracked for a fixed capability rather than per model, because models improve as they get cheaper. Exposure measures are compared by rank, because they are on different scales. Hiring is tested with an event study, because adoption has a date and a placebo can check it. The labour model separates what AI can do from what it costs, the two things the wage ceiling depends on. Market power is read from shares, because margins are not published. The macro models turn the measured parameters into scenarios, reported across the ranges the data cannot narrow.

3Methods and results

3.1 The price of intelligence

For each month t and capability threshold k, the price of capability is the lowest list price (input and output tokens weighted 3 to 1) among models whose Epoch Capabilities Index is at least k; 212 of Epoch’s 258 scored models match a price. A log-linear fit gives the halving time ln 2/|b| from ln pkt = a + bt. For demand I estimate

ln Qmt = β ln Pmt + αm + γt + εmt(2)

on OpenRouter model-months, so that β comes only from price changes within a model.

Results. The GPT-4-level price halves every 3.8 months, a fall of 89% a year (Figure 1); other thresholds give 2.6 to 4.4 months. A hedonic index that holds capability fixed ends at 14% of its September 2023 level [6%, 32%]. Token volume on OpenRouter rose 480-fold from January 2025 to November 2026 while the token-weighted price stayed near $1, because use moved to more capable models. But within a model, β = −0.47 [−1.03, 0.09] (58 models): price cuts do not show the elastic demand (|β| > 1) that a Jevons paradox would need. Aggregate growth reflects new users and uses, not proof of Jevons.

3.2 Where AI is used

I map Microsoft’s applicability score (what Copilot completed in 2024) and the Anthropic Economic Index (Claude usage, April and May 2026) to SOC occupations and compute rank correlations with bootstrap intervals, then regress the log usage ratio (usage share over employment share) on applicability with job-zone and major-group effects (robust errors). The measures agree moderately: ρ = 0.45 [0.38, 0.52] across 591 occupations, 0.28 within major groups and 0.80 [0.57, 0.91] across the 22 groups. Each 0.1 of applicability goes with a 2.1-fold usage ratio (coefficient 0.73, SE 0.12). Computer and mathematical jobs are 3.4% of US employment but 23.9% of usage; occupations scoring 0.3 or more hold 10.6% of the wage bill. More applicable jobs show more augmentation and less automation (ρ = −0.21). Exposure is real but concentrated.

3.3 Adoption in the economy

Firm adoption comes from the Census Business Trends and Outlook Survey. For hiring, I assign 830 occupations to Indeed’s 47 US job categories, compute employment-weighted exposure, and compare the most and least exposed thirds in an event study:

ln yct − ln ȳc2022 = Στ βτ Highc·1[t = τ] + δt + εct(3)

with errors clustered by category and a permutation test (10,000 draws).

Results. The share of firms using AI rose from 17.3% to 25.0% in a year (8.2 points [7.3, 9.1]), from 11.6% in transport to 47.8% in information, and sector adoption tracks exposure (ρ = 0.66). Postings in the most exposed third are 23.8% lower than in the least exposed [−34.9%, −10.7%; permutation p = 0.002], and the result survives dropping software (−21.5%) or all tech categories (−16.3%). But the gap opened from mid-2022, before ChatGPT (Figure 2), while interest rates rose and the post-pandemic hiring boom ended. Firms themselves report little displacement: 2.5% say AI cut headcount, 7.0% that it raised it. This is a pattern, not proof of cause.

3.4 The labour model

Tasks i run from −n to 1; tasks below 0 are new tasks only people can do. AI can do tasks with i ≥ 1 − A at cost R = ρw0(1 + ηu), which rises with the share u of tasks on AI (compute scarcity). Tasks combine with elasticity of substitution σ, and the wage sets the price of output to one:

w1−σ I(ia) + (1 − ia) R1−σ = 1,(4)
ia = max{1 − A, ln(R/w)/θ},(5)

where I(a) = (eka − e−kn)/k and k = θ(1 − σ); the labour share is s = w1−σI(ia). Figure 5 shows the mechanism.

Three results follow. (i) With free adoption and fixed labour supply, the real wage never falls below its no-AI level, but the labour share does. (ii) Once R < weθ(1−A), AI is cheaper on every task it can do (the corner): a lower price no longer changes who does what, and the share A and σ decide the outcome. (iii) Cheaper AI raises the labour share if σ < 1 and lowers it if σ > 1. With A = 18% (Microsoft applicability weighted by wage bill), a million tokens for an hour of work cost $0.09 at GPT-5 level against average US pay of $33.36, or 0.3%. Token prices are far below the ceiling; what binds is the all-in cost of checking and integrating AI work, a parameter of the model.

3.5 Market structure and power

The Herfindahl–Hirschman index HHI = 10,000 Σsj2 is computed over firms (model authors mapped to parents) from daily OpenRouter token shares. Under Cournot competition the average Lerner index is HHI/ε. For open-weights models served by several hosts, I regress the log lowest price on the log number of hosts with model and month fixed effects.

Results. The HHI fell from 3,072 in January 2025 (Anthropic 51%; 3.3 equal firms’ worth) to 992 in April 2026, then rose to 1,577 in October 2026 (DeepSeek 34%) (Figure 3). The leading firm changed five times in 23 months, and 26 firms reached a 1% share. At a demand elasticity of 2, the October HHI implies a Lerner index of 0.08. Doubling the hosts of an open-weights model goes with a 29% lower price (elasticity −0.49 [−0.63, −0.34], 62 models), as Bertrand competition predicts; closed models show no such effect (−0.04 [−0.11, 0.02]).

3.6 Macroeconomics and policy

Model A is a Solow model with output Y = B A KαX1−α and X = [(1−g)Lρ + gCρ]1/ρ, ρ = (σ−1)/σ, where C is AI compute, growing with a doubling time of 0.7 years that slows by 20% a year (Epoch data). It is calibrated to the Penn World Table (US labour share 58.8%). Model B adds ideas production with research automation (Romer, 1990; Jones, 1995); Model C splits income between workers and owners, who spend 0.95 and 0.58 of each dollar, and lets an automation tax fund a basic income. After 30 years the labour share is 59.5% at σ = 0.5, 58.9% at 1, 57.3% at 1.25, 48.6% at 2 and 37.3% at 3; wages rise in every case, because compute raises labour’s marginal product even when its share falls. With σ = 2.5, a 25% automation tax pays a basic income of 3.7% of GDP and narrows the demand gap from 2.7% to 2.0% of potential output. The welfare-best rate, however, ranges from 12% to 95% across plausible tax-base elasticities and inequality aversion. In the data the US labour share fell from 63.7% (2000) to 56.8% (2023), long before AI compute grew, so the decline cannot be tied to AI.

3.7 India

Use. Across 120 countries, log Claude usage per person rises one for one with log GDP per capita (elasticity 0.96 [0.88, 1.04], R2 = 0.81). India’s usage index, 0.305, is 71% of the predicted value, inside the normal scatter, and the gap vanishes once the services share of employment is controlled.

Exposure. I use the PLFS 2025 first-visit file (460,857 workers), treat three-digit NCO-2015 codes as ISCO-08 (99.97% of workers match), link them to US occupations through BLS crosswalks, and express exposure as a percentile of the US workforce. Shares are survey-weighted; pay regressions cluster by first-stage unit.

Results. The average Indian worker sits at the 28th percentile of US exposure (Figure 4); 70.6% are below the US bottom-third line and 6.3% above the top-third line, against 33% in the US. Exposure is concentrated: 14.0% of urban and 3.0% of rural workers, 26.4% of graduates and 2.7% of others, 19.2% of regular wage workers and 0.3% of casual workers are above the line. A job at the 75th percentile pays 65% more than one at the 25th, but 5.4% after controlling for education and 4.8% after state effects [about 3%, 6%]: the raw gap is mostly the return to schooling. In the most exposed third, 51% of regular wage workers have no written contract (68% in the least exposed third).

Why pay anyone? Because the price of AI is not what decides. In the data and in the models, pay depends on what AI cannot yet do (the share A), on comparative advantage while compute is scarce, on whether automated and human tasks are complements (σ < 1), on new tasks, and on who captures the gains. Price falls act mainly through what becomes feasible, not through the price of tasks already automated.

Distribution, not mass unemployment, is the main risk. With free adoption the models give higher real wages but a lower labour share when compute substitutes for labour. Owners spend less of each dollar, so a shift towards them can open a demand gap. Policy should therefore target the distribution of AI income, through broad ownership, taxation of AI rents and social insurance, while the efficient tax rate is too uncertain (12% to 95%) for a precise rule.

Competition passes on the gains. Concentration among AI sellers fell, then rose, and leadership changes often. Open-weights models served by many hosts price like Bertrand competitors. Competition policy that keeps open models, multiple hosts and switching easy turns cheap intelligence into consumer surplus rather than markups.

India. Low exposure is an effect of the job mix (farming and elementary work), not of protection. Exposure falls on urban, educated, regular wage workers, the base of India’s services exports, and half of them lack written contracts. Three applications follow: skills that complement AI in exposed formal jobs; portable social protection for workers in exposed jobs without contracts; and measurement, since the PLFS could add questions on tasks and AI use to monitor effects as they arrive.

5Limitations

Prices are list prices from one source; a spot check against archived OpenAI pages matched three of four. Usage comes from two platforms, and exposure measures where AI is used, not job loss. The hiring evidence is correlational and its timing is confounded. The models are calibrated scenarios, not forecasts; σ and A are uncertain, and the results are reported across their ranges. The India crosswalk assumes US task content for Indian job titles. OpenRouter covers one marketplace.
